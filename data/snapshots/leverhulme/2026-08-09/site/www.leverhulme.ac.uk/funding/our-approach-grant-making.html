Our approach to grant-making

The Trust seeks to fund outstanding scholarship, while maintaining a distinctive role within the research funding landscape

In assessing applications for funding, we use the following established criteria to prioritise work of outstanding scholarship:

  • Originality – the research achieves more than the incremental development of a single discipline
  • Importance – the work will enable further research or enquiry
  • Significance – the proposed research has relevance outside a single field, and is able to excite those working in other disciplines
  • Merit – the quality of the research design and methodology, and the suitability of the researchers and institution for the realisation of the proposed research objectives

Secondary criteria

A second set of criteria reflect the particular values of the Leverhulme Trust, and express the Trust Board’s aspiration that our funding maintains a distinctive role within the research funding landscape. We particularly welcome applications that:

  • reflect an individual’s personal vision, aspiration, or intellectual curiosity
  • take appropriate risks in setting and pursuing research objectives
  • enable a refreshing departure from established patterns of working – either for the individual, or for the discipline
  • transcend disciplinary boundaries

Supplementary criteria

Supplementary criteria may apply to specific grant schemes – please refer to the detailed guidance on the relevant scheme pages before beginning an application.

Further guidance on our remit

The above criteria imply that there are categories of proposal which should be seen as less suited for submission to the Trust. These would include bids to support the ‘continuing activity’ of established research groups or teams, and proposals from established groups working on subjects, areas and themes where the Research Councils have a significant interest.

‘Eligibility for Research Council funding’ does not of itself preclude an application to the Trust. The quality of the proposal and likely resulting scholarship should always be considered paramount. However, the Trust wishes to maintain a distinctive role for its own limited funds alongside the support from other funders. If a proposal is well suited to the style and priorities of the Research Councils then a convincing reason for seeking Trust support in particular should always be evident.

Many of the Trust’s schemes allow for, indeed encourage, the study of research problems that cross established disciplinary boundaries. This is an intended consequence of applying the key criteria mentioned. It does not therefore follow that ‘lack of cross-disciplinarity’ of itself precludes an application to the Trust. Here, as elsewhere in our approach to grant-making, the originality and quality of the intended study are paramount. However, the Trust does not work within a disciplinary framework, and we particularly welcome proposals that are not bound by conventional disciplinary understandings.

The Trust also has a distinct appetite for proposals that are willing to take appropriate risks. This may include the development of new theories, the bringing together of new ideas from different disciplines, the development of new methodologies or approaches, including where there is little or no preliminary data, or where the PI seeks to change direction in pursuing their research vision. While we welcome proposals that might be considered too risky for other funders, our reviewers will nevertheless, expect explicit discussion of the risks and for the applicant to offer suitable mitigation where that is possible.

The grant decision-making process

All grant applications to the Leverhulme Trust are subject to extensive peer review. The Board of Trustees makes the final decision about funding, based on recommendations from our reviewers, panels and committees.

The number and nature of the peer review depends on the value of the grant and the particular scheme. In total, the Trust relies on around 14,000 pieces of peer review per annum, and we are enormously grateful for the support we receive from the academic community to do this. Without academics being willing to undertake this work, the Trust would not be able to operate in the rigorous way that it does.

In addition to the expert panels of academics that we convene to assess particular grant rounds, the Trust also has two standing committees of academics who support our grant-making.

The Research Awards Advisory Committee. This consists of thirteen eminent scholars attached to various academic institutions in the UK, covering a wide range of fields of study.  The individuals are selected not only for their domain expertise but also for their experience working in interdisciplinary groups. The Trustees have delegated authority to the Committee to make grants for the majority of our Fellowship schemes and some other individual awards.

The Leverhulme Advisory Panel. This consists of some forty-five UK-based academic specialists who provide a range of advice to the Trust, primarily relating to our biggest scheme, the Research Project Grants. Given the Trust’s remit to support interdisciplinary research, panel members are also required to have breadth of expertise in addition to subject expertise.

Members of our expert panels and committees receive a stipend for their work, which reflects the volume and complexity of the work. We do not, however, remunerate reviewers for individual pieces of peer review, such as acting as a nominated reviewer. However, we do have a policy of trying to reduce the burden on any individual academic by ensuring that we do not ask the same reviewer too many times in a given period.

Feedback

The Leverhulme Trust only employs twenty-two people and seeks to minimise both its administration costs and the burden on our peer reviewers when undertaking their review. Given the large number of applications for schemes, it is not, therefore, possible to provide feedback to individual applicants regarding unsuccessful applications. For some schemes, we provide aggregate feedback on the common reasons why applications were unsuccessful in that particular grant round.
